# Demand situations and segment distinctions

Which differences between customers deserve a segment boundary, and how to express a segment so the next decision can use it.

Consumers: [segment-market](../.claude/skills/segment-market/SKILL.md) (drawing boundaries), [prioritize-opportunities](../.claude/skills/prioritize-opportunities/SKILL.md) (stating the situation an opportunity arises in).

## The unit of analysis is the situation, not the person

People buy when a situation makes their current way of getting something done inadequate. The same person can be in several situations: an inspector writing a report the evening after a job and an inspector onboarding a junior colleague face different needs, alternatives and purchase conditions. Segmenting by situation keeps those apart; segmenting by person merges them and produces averages that fit nobody.

This follows the jobs-to-be-done line of work (Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan, "Know Your Customers' 'Jobs to Be Done'", *Harvard Business Review*, September 2016): the circumstance and the progress sought explain the purchase better than customer attributes. Used here for the choice of segmentation basis, not as evidence about any market.

A situation description has four parts:

| Part | Question | Example (fictional fixture) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Circumstance | When and where does the need arise? | The evening after an inspection, report due to the client next morning |
| Progress sought | What does the customer want to be true afterwards? | A client-ready report sent without re-typing field notes |
| Current alternative | What do they do now? | Word-processor template filled from handwritten notes and phone photos |
| Purchase conditions | Who decides, with what money, how fast? | The inspector alone, from their own business account |

## When a difference earns a boundary

A difference earns a boundary only when it changes a commercial decision. Test each candidate distinction against the decisions downstream:

| The difference changes… | Example | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The trigger | A licensing deadline versus a slow season | Different moment and message for reaching them |
| The alternative | Paper notes versus a competing app | Different advantage chain and switching effort |
| The buyer | Inspector buys for themselves versus a firm owner buys for a team | Different purchase path, length and proof needed |
| The delivery | Single seat versus shared templates and review | Different offer scope and product requirement |
| The value | Time saved per report versus consistency across inspectors | Different charging unit and price basis |

If none of these changes, the difference is a description, not a boundary. Demographic or firmographic attributes (region, firm size, years in business) become boundaries when they mark one of these differences: firm size matters because a firm owner buys for a team, not because "small" and "large" are tidy labels.

## Contrast neighbours

Define each segment against its nearest neighbour. "Independent inspectors" means little until set against "multi-inspector firms": what changes is the buyer (self versus owner), the buying cycle (between two jobs versus a trial over several jobs) and the delivery (one seat versus team templates). Writing the contrast exposes boundaries that do no work and segments that are really one.

## Recognition criteria

A segment is usable when three consumers can work with it:

- **Evidence** can recognise a member from an observable indicator (works alone; signs their own reports).
- **Engagement** can reach members through a channel they actually use.
- **Product** can state what members need delivered.

A criterion that only the venture can see ("would value our approach") recognises nobody. A criterion proposed by the worker is marked `[proposed]` until Evidence tests it.

## Failure modes

| Failure | Signature | Correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demographic slicing | Segments differ only by size or region, with identical needs and buyers | Merge, or find the decision the attribute changes |
| Solution-shaped segment | "Inspectors who want tablet reports" | Restate as the situation and progress; the solution belongs to the offer |
| Forced exclusivity | Every customer assigned to exactly one segment | Allow overlap; state the boundary and its consequence |
| Proposal promoted to fact | A segment the worker proposed appears as if the owner or Evidence described it | Mark `[proposed]`; attribute only what the source contains |
| Exhaustive taxonomy | Ten segments, three of which inform any decision | Keep the segments that change a choice; note the rest as boundaries |

## Worked application

Illustrative fixture: fictional venture, not evidence.

Brindlewick's brief names inspectors using the app and firms asking about team accounts. The distinction that earns a boundary is the buyer and buying cycle: the brief says firm owners "decide slowly and want a trial on a few jobs first" [owner: "decide slowly and want a trial on a few jobs first"], while independent inspectors decide for themselves. Region does not earn a boundary: nothing in the material says inspectors in different regions need different reports. A third candidate, inspectors who currently pay for a competing report app, is a worker proposal: its alternative and switching effort differ, so it is listed as `[proposed]` with the recognition criterion "names a paid report tool when asked what they use", for Evidence to test.
